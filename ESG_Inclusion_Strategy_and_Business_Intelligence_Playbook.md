# ESG, inclusion and strategic opportunity

## Purpose and use

This playbook helps an organisation build an Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) model around how it creates value, depends on people and natural systems, affects them, and makes decisions. Inclusion is integrated into that model through participation, design, resource allocation, accountability and evidence of experience.

Use it in commercial businesses, public bodies, charities, educational institutions and membership organisations. Adapt the scale of analysis to the organisation's activities and impacts. For a public or charitable organisation, strategic value includes mission delivery, equitable access, public trust and responsible use of resources; revenue and investor returns are not universal objectives.

The intended output is an operating model with priorities, funded actions and feedback into strategy. A sustainability report is one product of that model.

This playbook extends the repository's [accessibility-to-ESG mapping](AMM%20to%20ESG.md), [resource overview](README.md) and [agent guidance](AGENTS.md). It preserves their emphasis on comparable experience, ethical measurement and accountable delivery. It broadens the view to the whole ESG model, with accessibility as an important capability within wider inclusion.

**Status and interpretation.** The workflows, templates and decision rules below are proposed management methods. They are not a formal ESG standard, certification, numeric maturity model or adopted organisational policy. Sources are distinguished from this playbook's synthesis in the [source register](#source-register). Reporting obligations, framework versions and legal applicability need checking for the organisation and reporting period before external use.

### Start here

1. Agree the principles and scope in sections 1–3.
2. Map impacts, dependencies and strategic opportunities in sections 4–6.
3. Establish the business intelligence and investment methods in sections 7–9.
4. Assign ownership and embed decisions into workflows in sections 10–12.
5. Use sections 13–16 for implementation, examples, templates and review.

The minimum viable model is one named executive owner, an accessible stakeholder listening route, a combined impact-and-opportunity register, a small dashboard, funded actions and a regular decision meeting. A small organisation can run these in a controlled spreadsheet. Complex organisations need shared definitions and federated ownership across business units.

## 1. Begin with first ESG principles

### 1.1 Define the three lenses

ESG groups related questions about organisational performance. The pillars interact, but each requires its own evidence.

| Lens | First-principles question | Typical scope | Inclusion question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Environmental | How do we depend on, affect and help sustain natural systems? | Climate, energy, water, pollution, materials, waste, biodiversity and resilience | Who receives environmental benefits, who bears costs or exposure, and who can participate in the transition? |
| Social | How do our activities affect people's rights, opportunities, safety and lived experience? | Own workforce, value-chain workers, customers, service users and affected communities | Can people participate, exercise choice and achieve equivalent value with dignity? |
| Governance | How do authority, incentives, controls and accountability produce responsible decisions? | Oversight, conduct, integrity, risk, procurement, data, reporting and remedy | Whose evidence influences decisions, who owns exclusion, and what happens when commercial pressure conflicts with commitments? |

ESG management, sustainability reporting and ESG ratings serve different purposes. Management changes decisions and operations. Reporting communicates selected information under defined requirements. Ratings apply a provider's methodology. A favourable rating cannot establish that every stakeholder has a good experience.

### 1.2 Set a foundation of responsible conduct

The UN Global Compact places human rights, labour, environment and anti-corruption at the foundation of corporate sustainability. It also makes clear that good practice in one area does not offset harm elsewhere. [S1]

Translate that foundation into six operating commitments:

1. **Respect rights and address harm.** Do not require a profitable business case before addressing discrimination, unsafe conditions or other serious rights impacts.
2. **Understand interdependence.** Identify the people, ecosystems, infrastructure and relationships on which delivery depends, including those outside direct organisational control.
3. **Create durable value.** Assess consequences over time and across the value chain, including costs transferred to customers, workers, communities or the environment.
4. **Share influence and opportunity.** Enable affected people to shape decisions and access the benefits of innovation and transition.
5. **Make evidence consequential.** Give each significant finding an owner, a decision, resources and a verification method.
6. **Tell the truth about performance.** Report progress, unresolved harm, uncertainty and exclusions. Keep ambition, implementation and verified outcomes distinct.

These six commitments are this playbook's proposed management synthesis. They are not a replacement wording for the source frameworks.

### 1.3 Use three connected assessments

| Assessment | What it asks | Decision it informs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Impact | What actual or potential effects do we have on people and the environment through activities and relationships? | Prevention, mitigation, remedy, design and impact reporting |
| Financial and organisational exposure | How might sustainability matters affect finances, continuity, capabilities or mission delivery? | Strategy, investment, resilience and enterprise risk |
| Opportunity | Where can a different product, service, operating model or partnership create better outcomes and durable value? | Innovation, market development, workforce development and resource allocation |

Keep these assessments connected without making one a prerequisite for another. Significant harm may need action without a near-term financial effect. An attractive innovation may need research before its impact or financial benefit is known.

GRI 3 focuses on an organisation's significant impacts on the economy, environment and people, including human rights. IFRS S1 focuses on sustainability-related risks and opportunities that could reasonably affect cash flows, access to finance or cost of capital. These perspectives have different audiences and materiality purposes. [S2][S3]

EFRAG's 2024 implementation guidance explains the ESRS distinction between impact and financial materiality; a matter can be material through either perspective. It is non-authoritative implementation guidance. This playbook borrows the discipline of examining both perspectives, without claiming ESRS conformity or asserting that every organisation is legally required to report under it. [S4]

## 2. Define meaningful inclusion

Inclusion means people can participate, influence decisions, access opportunities and achieve valuable outcomes. Accessibility enables participation by addressing barriers in environments, information, products and services. Representation helps reveal whose experience is missing. Equity recognises that people may need different support or resources to obtain fair opportunities.

Consider disability and neurodivergence alongside age, sex and gender, race and ethnicity, income, language, geography, caring responsibilities and other relevant circumstances. Determine legal categories locally. Explore intersections where the question and sample support them; do not demand comprehensive identity disclosure to use a service or take part in research.

Barriers also arise from situations: fatigue, pain, stress, bereavement, emergency, low connectivity, noisy surroundings, low digital confidence or limited financial capacity. A person can encounter different barriers across a day or over their life. Design around needs and tasks without assuming that a demographic label predicts individual capability.

### 2.1 Evaluate equivalent value

The repository defines comparable experience through outcomes, effort, dignity, confidence and time. Use these as separate dimensions:

| Dimension | Practical question | Evidence to gather |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outcome | Can the person achieve the intended result and obtain equivalent value? | Task success, entitlement received, product usefulness, work or learning outcome |
| Effort | What extra cognitive, physical, administrative or financial burden occurs? | Steps, repeated explanations, visits, workload ratings and direct costs |
| Dignity | Does the process preserve privacy, agency and respectful treatment? | Accessible interviews, complaints and observation of service interactions |
| Confidence | Does the person understand the choice and trust their ability to proceed? | Comprehension checks and participant accounts |
| Time | Are delays and time demands reasonable for the task and context? | Completion and waiting times, including pauses and required support |

Agree context-specific acceptance criteria with affected people. Identical speed is not always an appropriate objective. Assistance chosen by the person can enable an excellent experience; compulsory dependence caused by a barrier should remain visible. Record unassisted success, chosen assistance and barrier-required assistance separately.

Technical accessibility tests, workforce representation and satisfaction each contribute evidence. None alone establishes equivalent experience. Satisfaction can be affected by low expectations, fear of consequences or an inaccessible survey. Triangulate it with observed outcomes and qualitative accounts.

### 2.2 Give participation influence

The Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities provides a rights foundation, including accessibility, work and participation. Article 4(3) addresses States Parties' consultation with and active involvement of disabled people. The organisational practice proposed here is to involve disabled people and other affected groups in decisions, without treating the treaty as a direct, uniform corporate reporting obligation. [S5]

Provide accessible recruitment and information, appropriate compensation, communication options and time to participate. Include people excluded from current channels, applicants who withdrew, former customers and workers in relevant supply chains. Explain what changed because of participation and why some proposals were not adopted. Employee networks and champions can connect people and surface patterns; they need protected time and access to decision-makers, while executives retain accountability.

## 3. Establish the organisational model

### 3.1 Write a one-page ESG charter

Agree the following before choosing dashboard indicators:

| Charter element | Required decision |
| --- | --- |
| Purpose and value | What the organisation exists to deliver; what durable value means for it |
| Boundary | Entities, locations, services, workforce arrangements and upstream/downstream relationships included |
| People and nature | Who may be affected; resources and ecosystems on which delivery depends |
| Commitments | Rights, environmental responsibilities, inclusion intent and relevant contractual or legal duties |
| Time horizons | Define short, medium and long term to fit planning, assets and consequences; explain differences across matters |
| Authority | Executive sponsor, governing-body oversight, service ownership, funding authority and escalation |
| Evidence and voice | How affected people participate; data sources, privacy conditions and evidence limits |
| Reporting | Intended users, applicable frameworks, organisational boundary, period and approval route |

Include outsourced work and downstream use where relevant. Lack of direct control is a reason to define influence and leverage, rather than automatically excluding an impact.

### 3.2 Connect strategy to evidence

Use a shared chain of records:

**Purpose → dependency or impact → strategic objective → intervention → implemented control → experience and environmental outcomes → verified value → next decision.**

Assign stable identifiers so a board claim can be traced to actions, evidence and limitations. One intervention can affect several ESG topics; link it to each while recording its cost and benefits once in the portfolio.

Maintain four separate views: commitments, implemented capability, outcomes and evidence quality. A policy can exist while delivery fails. Outcomes can improve while evidence remains too weak for a broad claim.

## 4. Discover what matters

### 4.1 Map the value chain and critical journeys

Map inputs, suppliers, workforce, operations, distribution, use, support and end of life. For service organisations, include information, eligibility, onboarding, everyday use, complaints, renewal and exit. For employers, include attraction, recruitment, onboarding, development, progression, retention and departure.

At each stage ask:

- What resources, capabilities and relationships enable delivery?
- Who can be excluded, injured, exploited or exposed to environmental harm?
- What costs, delays or workarounds are transferred to other people?
- What changes under growth, automation, ageing, climate disruption or financial pressure?
- What could we redesign to improve participation and value?

Review evidence with people who operate the process and people who experience it. Include commercial, finance, procurement, technology, property, HR, research and environmental expertise as relevant.

### 4.2 Create an impact, dependency, risk and opportunity register

Use one linked register with distinct record types. Do not turn an impact on people into only a reputational risk to the organisation.

| Field | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| ID and type | Impact, dependency, risk or opportunity; link related entries |
| Scope and relationship | Activity, journey, location, population, time horizon; caused, contributed to or directly linked where applicable |
| Mechanism | How the effect arises; distinguish observation from hypothesis |
| Consequence | Rights, environment, lived experience, operational, financial or mission effect |
| Evidence | Source, period, method, quality, contradiction and missing perspectives |
| Significance | Severity, reach, reversibility and likelihood where appropriate; document judgement |
| Current response | Existing controls, leverage, effectiveness and residual problem |
| Opportunity | Alternative approach, beneficiaries, value mechanism and possible adverse effects |
| Decision | Owner, proposed action, resources, due date, verification and review |

Human-rights due diligence should consider risks to affected people, and remedy where the enterprise causes or contributes to harm. UN Guiding Principles 17–22 distinguish identification, integration, tracking, communication and remediation. Principle 24 prioritises the most severe impacts or those for which delay would make remedy impossible. [S6]

### 4.3 Prioritise without erasing serious harm

Use separate decisions for:

1. **Immediate protection and obligations:** serious harm, essential-service exclusion, unsafe work, sensitive-data exposure and other urgent duties.
2. **Systemic improvement:** recurring barriers, weak controls, environmental impacts and structural disadvantages.
3. **Strategic opportunity:** new value propositions, partnerships and operating models worth testing.

In impact assessment, distinguish actual harm from potential harm. Assess gravity, extent and difficulty of remedy; likelihood applies to potential impacts. Severity takes precedence for potential negative human-rights impacts. Do not make a small affected population an automatic low priority. [S2][S6]

Approve and record significance criteria and thresholds. Stakeholder voting, survey popularity and financial return alone cannot determine impact significance. Keep dissenting views and uncertainty visible. Missing information is an investigation need, rather than evidence that a topic is immaterial.

**Output:** a reasoned list of significant matters, urgent actions, investigation needs and opportunity hypotheses. Management sequencing does not remove a material matter from a reporting assessment.

## 5. Build a strategic opportunity portfolio

Use business intelligence to identify a mechanism for creating value, rather than attaching an inclusion label to an existing initiative. Each opportunity should improve a specified experience or environmental outcome and explain how that change supports organisational purpose.

### 5.1 Opportunity discovery map

The entries below are hypotheses to investigate. They are not promises of benefit.

| Opportunity | Inclusion mechanism | Business intelligence to examine | Potential strategic value | Test and guardrail |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Reach underserved customers or beneficiaries | Remove access, language, cost or channel barriers | Abandoned journeys, eligible non-users, research with excluded people, demand and competitor gaps | Additional suitable demand; wider mission reach | Test ability to obtain value and informed choice; avoid exploitative targeting |
| Improve retention and trust | Reduce recurring friction and resolve harm | Cohort retention, complaints, cancellation reasons, experience research | Lower avoidable loss; stronger relationships | Separate service effects from price, product mix and selection bias |
| Strengthen workforce capability | Accessible recruitment, adjustments, learning and progression | Withdrawal, adjustment delays, progression, retention and workload | Broader talent access; retained expertise; reliable delivery | Protect disclosure and worker voice; avoid productivity surveillance |
| Improve operating quality | Fix root causes instead of repeated workarounds | Repeat contacts, rework, failure demand, staff observations | More usable capacity and fewer defects | Confirm that lower contact volume reflects success, rather than abandonment |
| Develop inclusive products | Co-design around unmet tasks and contextual barriers | Task research, unmet demand, prototype outcomes, willingness and ability to pay | Differentiation; useful innovation; transferable design capability | Demonstrate value and affordability before scaling |
| Enable a fair environmental transition | Accessible repair, reuse, transport, retrofit or energy services | Uptake, exclusion reasons, cost burden, location and environmental data | Wider participation; stronger transition delivery | Measure environmental results separately; check burden on affected people |
| Strengthen supply continuity | Improve supplier capability and working conditions | Concentration, defects, worker feedback, payment terms and incident patterns | Resilience, quality and access to capable smaller suppliers | Support improvement; avoid shifting unaffordable requirements downstream |
| Improve procurement and bids | Produce traceable evidence of delivery capability | Buyer requirements, lost bids, implementation costs, reference outcomes | Better-qualified bids; credible partnerships | A documented capability does not guarantee contract wins |
| Build community legitimacy | Share influence over local impacts and benefits | Accessible engagement, grievances, local exposure and benefit distribution | Better decisions; durable relationships; mission effectiveness | Consultation must influence decisions and allow disagreement |
| Improve capital stewardship | Integrate sustainability dependencies into investment | Asset exposure, scenarios, costs, financing criteria and verified outcomes | Better allocation and resilience | Do not assume inclusion automatically lowers financing costs |

### 5.2 Turn a finding into a strategic hypothesis

Use this form:

> For **[people and task]**, **[barrier or unmet need]** causes **[observed consequence]**. Changing **[design, policy or operating model]** is expected to improve **[experience or environmental outcome]**, supporting **[financial or mission value]** through **[mechanism]**. We will test **[uncertain assumption]**, protect against **[harm]**, and decide **[scale, adapt or stop]** using **[evidence and date]**.

Require a plausible mechanism, a named decision-maker and a way to falsify the hypothesis. “Inclusion improves reputation” is too vague until it identifies whose trust, what behaviour might change and how that will be investigated.

### 5.3 Balance the portfolio

Fund urgent protection and required remediation as responsibilities. Alongside these, maintain a portfolio of improvements with near-term operational value, enabling capabilities with longer-term value and exploratory innovation. Do not make rights protection compete on the same return ranking as discretionary innovation.

For discretionary options, compare expected user benefit, financial or mission value, strategic fit, feasibility, time to learn, uncertainty and adverse effects. Use a documented qualitative comparison or an explicitly approved weighting method. This is investment appraisal, not an ESG maturity score. Retain important dimensions separately so a strong commercial forecast cannot cancel severe harm.

## 6. Integrate inclusion across Environmental, Social and Governance

### 6.1 Environmental: participation, distribution and measured ecological performance

Keep a genuine environmental programme covering the significant matters identified for the organisation. Inclusion adds questions about access, distribution and transition; it does not replace environmental expertise.

| Environmental matter | Inclusion integrated into the decision | Environmental evidence | Experience and distribution evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Climate mitigation and energy | Accessible energy advice, retrofit, travel and low-carbon product choices | Energy use and emissions under a stated accounting method and boundary | Uptake, affordability, task success and who bears transition costs |
| Adaptation and continuity | Accessible warnings, evacuation, cooling and alternative service routes | Location-specific hazards, exposure, resilience measures and incident outcomes | Reach, comprehension and ability to act under disruption |
| Circular materials and waste | Repairable products, accessible returns and usable reuse schemes | Material flows, waste treatment, product life and relevant lifecycle evidence | Ability to repair or return, transport burden and cost |
| Water and pollution | Account for workers and communities affected by scarcity or exposure | Location-specific withdrawals, discharges and pollutant evidence | Distribution of exposure, access to information and effective remedy |
| Nature and land use | Accessible engagement with affected communities while respecting local rights | Dependencies, ecosystem impacts, location and changes in condition | Participation, benefit distribution, livelihood effects and unresolved concerns |

GHG Protocol provides methods for corporate greenhouse-gas inventories; use the relevant Corporate, Scope 2 and Scope 3 guidance for the chosen inventory. More digital transactions or fewer office visits do not by themselves establish a net emissions reduction. [S7]

For significant nature-related issues, TNFD's LEAP approach supports locating interfaces with nature, evaluating dependencies and impacts, assessing risks and opportunities, and preparing responses. [S8]

The ILO identifies disability inclusion as a dimension of a just transition, including skills, social protection and enabling conditions for decent work. [S9] Apply this by testing whether green jobs, training, infrastructure and benefits are accessible, and whether transition changes remove existing support or impose disproportionate burdens.

Record trade-offs explicitly. For example, a travel policy may reduce emissions while restricting an employee's accessible transport choices. Compare feasible alternatives, protect participation, and measure the actual environmental consequences. Accessibility supports equitable environmental participation; a carbon-saving claim needs separate calculation and evidence.

### 6.2 Social: extend beyond the customer interface

| Population | Strategic questions | Example actions | Outcome evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Own workforce | Who can enter, develop, influence and remain in work? | Accessible hiring, timely adjustments, fair progression and inclusive learning | Withdrawal, retention, progression, adjustment effectiveness, safety and voice |
| Value-chain workers | What do purchasing and delivery practices mean for working conditions? | Worker-informed due diligence, realistic lead times and corrective action | Grievances resolved, safer conditions and worker accounts of change |
| Customers and service users | Who can understand choices, obtain value and leave or complain? | Accessible communications, usable services, affordable support and fair exit | Comparable experience, comprehension, suitable uptake, complaints and retention |
| Affected communities | Who experiences local costs, displacement, exposure or benefit? | Early engagement, locally appropriate access, benefit design and remedy | Distribution of outcomes and affected people's assessment |

Consider decent work, discrimination, health and safety, fair treatment, privacy and access together. A reduction in complaints can reflect fear or inaccessibility; representation can increase while progression remains blocked. Investigate the mechanism behind the number.

### 6.3 Governance: make commitments survive pressure

Integrate inclusion into decision rights, budgets, incentives, procurement and assurance. Examine business conduct, anti-corruption, conflicts of interest, responsible influence and supplier relationships alongside accessibility governance.

The OECD's responsible-business-conduct cycle links policy and management systems, impact assessment, prevention and mitigation, tracking, communication and appropriate remediation. Use this as a recurring management discipline. [S10]

Every significant issue needs an accountable human owner with authority to act. A release exception or investment trade-off should record affected people, alternatives, immediate safeguards, residual harm, resources, expiry and verification. An internal exception does not waive legal obligations. Champions and AI agents can surface evidence; they cannot absorb executive accountability.

## 7. Build business intelligence for decisions

Business intelligence (BI) means turning data and experience into information that changes a decision. Begin with a question and decision owner, then collect the minimum evidence needed.

### 7.1 Design a decision-led evidence pipeline

| Stage | Required practice | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Question | Identify the choice, owner, deadline and stakes | Should we redesign eligibility and onboarding before expanding the service? |
| Source | Combine operational, financial, environmental and lived-experience evidence | Application events, support contacts, costs, accessible interviews and energy data |
| Quality | Check definitions, missing channels, representativeness, lineage and freshness | Determine whether abandoned applications are absent from the survey |
| Analysis | Examine distributions, group differences, trends and alternative explanations | Compare task outcomes by channel and voluntarily reported access needs |
| Interpretation | Separate finding, hypothesis, uncertainty and proposed action | Errors cluster at identity verification; the causal mechanism needs observation |
| Decision | Record action, funding, owner and acceptance criteria | Pilot an accessible verification route with privacy controls |
| Verification | Check implementation and experience, then reallocate resources | Review task outcomes, burden, costs and unintended effects before scaling |

Use public statistics, regulator findings, climate and nature data, labour-market information, sector research and buyer requirements for external context. Record geography, period, definitions and relevance. Do not apply a global prevalence statistic as the measured composition of a particular workforce or customer base.

### 7.2 Assemble complementary evidence

| Evidence family | Useful inputs | Blind spot to address |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Commercial or mission | Demand, revenue, margin, reach, retention, eligibility and service outcomes | People never reached or unable to enter the funnel |
| Operational | Task attempts, failure demand, delays, rework, adjustments and service incidents | Quiet abandonment, workarounds and invisible effort |
| Experience | Accessible surveys, interviews, observation, complaints and participatory research | Convenience samples, low expectations, fear and inaccessible instruments |
| Workforce and supplier | Progression, withdrawal, worker voice, conditions and contract performance | Non-disclosure, contractor exclusion and fear of retaliation |
| Environmental | Inventory activity data, locations, material flows and ecological dependencies | Estimates, boundary changes, externalised impacts and rebound effects |
| Governance | Decisions, budgets, exceptions, action closure and independent review | Paper compliance and activities without implementation |

Collect both negative and positive evidence. Retain contradictory findings for investigation rather than discarding the source that is less favourable.

### 7.3 Protect people while learning

Use voluntary self-description where appropriate. Give people an undisclosed or prefer-not-to-say option and retain it as a distinct group. Never treat non-disclosure as non-disability. Allow access preferences to be expressed without demanding diagnosis, while recognising that preferences can still reveal sensitive information.

Do not infer disability, health or vulnerability from assistive-technology use, click patterns, typing, voice, names or other proxies. Do not join survey responses to customer or employee accounts merely because a login makes it possible. Anonymous collection can still be identifying through small groups, free text or linkage.

For UK GDPR processing, the ICO explains that special-category data needs both an Article 6 lawful basis and an Article 9 condition. Intentionally inferred sensitive characteristics can also be special-category data. [S11] Determine applicable local requirements with the privacy lead before collection. Voluntary participation and consent to research do not automatically establish every legal condition for processing.

Approve purpose, access, separation of identities, retention and deletion. Assess high-risk processing appropriately. Restrict raw disclosures, redact free text, and apply suppression rules that also prevent reconstruction through totals and overlapping groups. Keep research disclosure separate from employment, pricing, credit or individual eligibility decisions unless a distinct, legitimate and approved purpose requires otherwise.

Measurement must be accessible. Offer appropriate modes, usable language and time; test the instrument with intended participants. Do not assume a generic psychometric or satisfaction scale is valid across populations. Analyse non-response and record who the method cannot reach.

### 7.4 Analyse carefully

- Show absolute values, denominators, distributions and uncertainty alongside headline rates. Use percentage points for differences between percentages.
- Compare equivalent tasks and contexts; account for channel, product, location, seasonality and changing user mix.
- Include unsuccessful attempts, abandonment and people who never reach completion surveys. Explain unavoidable exclusions.
- A small sample may reveal a serious barrier while remaining unsuitable for prevalence estimates. Use qualitative findings for the questions they can answer.
- Use a suitable evaluation design: phased rollout, matched comparison or controlled experiment where appropriate. Never withhold necessary accessibility or protection in order to create an experiment.
- A before-and-after change can be informative without proving causation. Test alternative explanations and distinguish association from attribution.
- Investigate whether AI or automation shifts burden onto people, hides failures or reduces access to human review. Log model and method changes affecting results.

## 8. Select a balanced set of measures

Select a small set for each strategic objective. Expand only where a decision requires it. Targets should follow a baseline, affected-person input, relevant obligations and organisational ambition; the examples below do not prescribe universal thresholds.

| Measure | Definition or method | Interpretation and decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Successful participation | Successful eligible attempts / all eligible attempts observed × 100; define success and treatment of abandonment | Segment by approved groups and channel; investigate who is missing from observation |
| Completion gap | Reference-group success rate minus specified-group success rate, in percentage points | State both rates and sample limits; a negative gap is possible; use a defined reference group |
| Comparable experience | Separate assessment of outcome, effort, dignity, confidence and time in matched task contexts | Improve deficient dimensions; do not infer all-dimensional equivalence from success alone |
| Experience and satisfaction | Distribution on a defined, accessible instrument, plus accounts of experience | Link negative signals to action; record response rate and selection limitations |
| Adjustment effectiveness | Completed adjustments followed up within an agreed window, assessed by the person and task outcome | Track elapsed time and unresolved needs; closure of a ticket is insufficient |
| Opportunity conversion | Stage progression in a defined hiring, learning, service or procurement funnel | Compare like stages and contexts; investigate barriers rather than diagnosing people |
| Failure demand | Contacts or rework attributable to a defined service failure / relevant activity volume | Distinguish reduced failure from reduced help-seeking; validate coding through case review |
| Verified remediation | Actions due in the period verified by their deadline / all actions due in the period × 100 | Keep overdue carry-over, severity and recurrence visible separately |
| Environmental participation | Eligible people obtaining the intended sustainability benefit / eligible people within the defined measured scope | Analyse access, affordability and distribution; distinguish uptake from ecological benefit |
| Environmental performance | Topic-specific physical measures under a stated method and boundary | Show relevant absolute and intensity measures; investigate effects transferred elsewhere |
| Verified organisational value | Realised financial benefit or mission outcome compared with a defined baseline or counterfactual | Separate evidence from forecast and cash from capacity; prevent duplicate attribution |
| Governance effectiveness | Evidence-backed review of ownership, resourcing, decision follow-through and exception handling | Diagnose why commitments fail; meeting counts alone do not establish effectiveness |

For every metric record: ID, objective, scope, population, period, method version, formula, unit, numerator, denominator, baseline, target, due date, result, sample, missing data, uncertainty, segmentation, evidence references, owner, freshness limit, collection frequency, review frequency and privacy approval where relevant. Use the repository's [measurement contract](AGENTS.md#measurement-contract) as the detailed pattern.

Keep evidence states explicit: sufficient for the stated decision, partial, missing, stale or conflicting. Unknown is not zero. A zero denominator requires a reasoned not-applicable or insufficient-data result. A suppressed result is not a pass. Do not invent confidence percentages or an overall ESG score.

### 8.1 Design the executive dashboard around action

For each significant matter, show:

1. The commitment and strategic question.
2. The affected scope and important distributional differences.
3. Baseline, target, latest result and trend.
4. Evidence status and limitations.
5. Serious unresolved harm, exceptions and overdue actions.
6. Investment, verified value and decisions required.
7. Accountable owner and next review.

Show favourable and adverse movement together. Provide accessible tables and text explanations for charts. Do not rely on colour alone or combine unrelated outcomes into a green headline.

## 9. Make investment and business cases credible

### 9.1 Specify the value mechanism

| Value type | Possible calculation | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Additional commercial contribution | Incremental completed transactions × contribution per transaction, less incremental operating costs | Use contribution rather than revenue; validate demand, displacement and attribution |
| Retained value | Credibly attributable reduction in avoidable loss × retained contribution over the stated period | Avoid counting the same customer in acquisition and retention benefits |
| Cash saving | Costs actually removed from budgets, net of implementation and ongoing costs | Staff time released is not cash saved unless spend changes |
| Capacity released | Verified hours no longer spent on failure × relevant capacity value | State how capacity will be used; keep separate from cash savings |
| Resilience | Scenario-specific continuity, disruption or loss estimates | State probability assumptions and uncertainty; do not present avoided losses as realised income |
| Mission value | More suitable beneficiaries served, better outcomes or reduced burden per unit of resource | Avoid arbitrary monetisation of dignity or rights |
| Environmental value | Measured resource or emissions change under the relevant method | Participation rates and financial savings are not environmental units |

Include research, participation, procurement, adjustments, training, integration, support, assurance and ongoing maintenance in costs. Test low, central and high assumptions for discretionary investment. Label forecasts and record whose judgement supports them. Use the organisation's approved appraisal method and discount rate for multi-year cases.

### 9.2 Worked financial illustration

**Hypothetical planning example; not evidence about any organisation.** A service has 20,000 eligible attempts a year, 60% successful completion and £15 contribution per completed transaction. A proposed redesign costs £30,000 initially and £8,000 annually.

| Scenario | Assumed improvement | Additional completions | Additional annual contribution | Annual contribution after ongoing cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Low | 3 percentage points | 600 | £9,000 | £1,000 |
| Central | 6 percentage points | 1,200 | £18,000 | £10,000 |
| High | 10 percentage points | 2,000 | £30,000 | £22,000 |

These simplified cases assume stable volume and contribution, incremental completions, and no other variable-cost changes. Initial cost is excluded from the annual net column. Under steady-state assumptions, central-case simple payback is £30,000 / £10,000 = three years; low-case payback is 30 years. Neither accounts for rollout, discounting or timing. The purpose is to expose uncertainty and decide what to learn before scaling.

If the redesign removes a serious exclusion or meets an obligation, a weak commercial return does not remove that responsibility. The appraisal can help choose effective delivery options and sequence investment, while the required outcome remains explicit.

## 10. Assign governance and decision rights

One person may hold several roles in a small organisation. Preserve a clear accountable owner and arrange proportionate independent challenge for consequential claims and conflicts of interest.

| Decision or activity | Accountable human role | Delivery and challenge |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ESG charter, priorities and resource envelope | Governing body or designated executive | Strategy, finance, ESG, affected representatives and specialists |
| Impact and opportunity assessment | Executive sponsor | Service owners, risk, research, environmental and inclusion expertise |
| Service or workforce improvement | Relevant business/service owner | Product, engineering, content, operations, HR or property teams |
| Evidence collection and analysis | Named evidence owner | Research, BI, privacy and domain specialists; affected participants |
| Investment approval | Delegated budget holder | Finance tests assumptions; owners demonstrate outcome mechanisms |
| Material harm and remedy | Relevant executive or service owner | Incident, legal, worker/community representatives and domain specialists |
| External ESG claim | Designated reporting approver | Evidence owners, finance, legal where needed and independent review |
| Model and source maintenance | Repository or methodology owner | Specialists check changes, definitions and continued fitness |

### 10.1 Review cadence

Propose monthly operational reviews and quarterly executive reviews, adapted to scale and risk. Align major portfolio and reporting decisions to the planning cycle. Review immediately after serious harm, major acquisitions, outsourcing, automation, service changes, environmental incidents or new reporting requirements.

At each review ask: what changed, who benefited, who remains excluded, what is uncertain, what will we fund or stop, and who verifies the result? Track unresolved actions between meetings. Serious incidents must not wait for the regular review.

### 10.2 Incentives, escalation and remedy

Avoid incentives that reward closing tickets, reducing contacts or increasing disclosure without checking outcomes. Give leaders responsibility for both delivery and unresolved consequences.

Use an accessible grievance route that offers confidentiality, support, alternatives and protection against retaliation. Involve affected people in assessing appropriate remedy. Correct the person's situation where possible and fix the systemic cause; financial compensation alone may not restore access or trust.

Escalate material exclusion, suspected rights harm, privacy breaches, unsupported claims and repeated overdue remediation to the appropriate owner. Record severity, immediate safeguards, evidence, deadlines, decision, residual harm and verification in a restricted system. Public repositories should contain guidance and redacted examples, rather than personal case records.

## 11. Embed the model in everyday workflows

| Workflow | When inclusion and ESG evidence are useful | Required decision or artefact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strategy and annual planning | Before objectives and budgets are fixed | Significant matters, opportunity portfolio, dependencies and funded outcomes |
| Product and service discovery | Before requirements harden | Unmet tasks, affected-person input, environmental consequences and testable hypothesis |
| Architecture and design | Before costly structural decisions | Access routes, interoperability, information needs, privacy and failure recovery |
| Engineering and content | During creation and change | Accessible patterns, clear content, automated and manual checks, owned defects |
| Quality assurance and research | Throughout development; outcome evaluation when known barriers are addressed | Technical evidence plus lived experience of real tasks; user testing must not substitute for routine QA |
| Procurement and supplier management | Before award and throughout the contract | Evidence requirements, accessible evaluation, worker/environmental due diligence and remediation commitments |
| Workforce and learning | At entry, change, progression and departure | Accessible processes, effective adjustments, opportunity distribution and worker voice |
| Operations and support | At service delivery and incident review | Accessible help, continuity, root-cause analysis, remedy and recurrence monitoring |
| Marketing and communications | Before propositions, campaigns and ESG claims | Promise-to-experience check, understandable choices and bounded evidence |
| Finance and investment | Before funding; after implementation | Option appraisal, total costs, uncertainty and verified benefits |
| Property, logistics and transition | Before physical change and environmental investment | Accessible use, safety, distributional effects and environmental measurement |
| Mergers, acquisitions and outsourcing | During due diligence and integration | Inherited barriers, liabilities, dependencies, capability gaps and integration budget |

Use AI agents to retrieve approved sources, identify evidence gaps, prepare comparisons and monitor authorised records. Retain human accountability for materiality, sensitive processing, significant trade-offs and public claims. Agents must preserve source versions and uncertainty and must not invent approvals, infer disability or self-certify maturity. Apply the repository's [agent guidance](AGENTS.md) to those uses.

## 12. Report and assure what the model demonstrates

### 12.1 Keep reporting frameworks distinct

| Source or framework | Useful contribution | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| UN Global Compact and UN Guiding Principles | Principles, rights responsibilities and due diligence | Referencing them does not establish membership, endorsement or verified conformity |
| OECD responsible-business-conduct guidance | Recurring management of impacts and remedy | It is not a universal ESG rating methodology |
| GRI | Impact-oriented material topics and management reporting | Use current requirements before making a reporting-conformity claim |
| IFRS S1 | Sustainability-related financial disclosure, including governance, strategy, risk management, metrics and targets | Its investor-focused materiality differs from an all-stakeholder impact assessment |
| ESRS and EFRAG implementation guidance | Double-materiality concepts and implementation support | Confirm current rules, versions and organisational applicability separately |
| GHG Protocol | Defined greenhouse-gas accounting methods | It does not establish social outcomes or verify every environmental claim |
| TNFD | Nature-related dependencies, impacts, risks and opportunities | Nature assessment needs relevant locations, expertise and evidence |
| This repository and playbook | Inclusion, comparable experience and management integration | Neither is a formal ESG reporting standard or certification scheme |

Use a reporting crosswalk from existing evidence to framework requirements. Do not relabel an internally useful dashboard as a compliant external report. Confirm boundaries, periods, required disclosures and assurance expectations for each intended use. Frameworks and laws change; this document does not fix their applicability as of every future reporting period.

### 12.2 Match claims to evidence

| Evidence available | Defensible form of statement | Further evidence needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Policy and named owner | A commitment and accountability arrangement have been established | Implementation and outcomes |
| Training and implementation records | Specified practices were delivered within the stated scope | Effectiveness and experience |
| Defined research and operational results | A bounded finding for the observed task, period and participants | Broader reach, durability and other experience dimensions |
| Controlled outcome verification | An outcome was verified under the stated method and scope | Any broader or causal claim beyond that method |
| Participation in an environmental service | Access or uptake improved within scope | Environmental change measured under a suitable method |

Example: “In the June–September pilot, 43 of 50 participating users completed the application, compared with 30 of 50 in the earlier study. These were separate recruited samples, so the result does not establish a causal effect or population-wide equivalence. Participants continued to report difficulty recovering from verification errors; the service owner has a funded action due in December.”

This hypothetical wording reports progress and limits together. It cannot be shortened to “all customers now have an equivalent experience”.

Separate management review, independent internal challenge and external assurance. Describe which occurred, its scope and limitations. A documented process or automated check is not independent assurance. If evidence is stale, missing or conflicting, narrow or hold the affected claim and investigate. Correct published errors transparently.

## 13. Launch in 90 days, then operate continuously

The schedule below is an adaptable implementation sequence. Address urgent harm immediately, regardless of phase.

| Period | Work | Concrete output and decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Days 1–15 | Establish sponsor, scope, principles and participation routes; identify urgent harm | Draft charter, owners, immediate safeguards and initial evidence requests |
| Days 16–30 | Map value chain, critical journeys, dependencies and significant matters | Baseline register, evidence gaps, priority rationale and initial listening findings |
| Days 31–45 | Define metrics, approve data handling and examine strategic hypotheses | Metric contracts, privacy conditions and a short opportunity portfolio |
| Days 46–60 | Appraise options, fund remediation and design pilots | Resourced actions, test plans, acceptance criteria and budget decisions |
| Days 61–75 | Implement and evaluate initial changes | Delivery evidence, outcome findings, unintended effects and revised assumptions |
| Days 76–90 | Review findings and integrate with planning and reporting | Decision to scale, adapt or stop each pilot; dashboard and next-quarter plan |

Some outcomes, especially environmental, workforce and long-term trust outcomes, cannot be verified within 90 days. Establish a credible baseline and follow-up rather than manufacturing a result to meet the timetable.

Continue to discover, act, verify and learn. Review source versions at least at the scheduled document review and whenever a relevant framework changes. Review the organisational model after material changes to the business or its impacts.

### 13.1 Scale the method to the organisation

| Context | Proportionate implementation | Keep intact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small organisation | One combined register, a few critical journeys, existing records, accessible interviews and a short quarterly review | Clear ownership, privacy, serious-harm response and evidence-backed claims |
| Medium organisation | Shared metric definitions, cross-functional review, a portfolio budget and periodic independent challenge | Value-chain view and feedback from affected people |
| Large or complex organisation | Federated service ownership, common evidence model, specialist assessments and consolidated oversight | Local context, distributional differences and traceability to source evidence |
| Public or charitable body | Prioritise mission, access, eligibility, public value and stewardship alongside financial sustainability | Ability to complain, appeal and obtain support without digital exclusion |

## 14. Apply the model in different settings

The examples are hypothetical starting points. Each needs local assessment.

| Setting | Strategic question and intervention | Paired evidence | Decision and guardrail |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Small professional-services firm | Can accessible proposals, recruitment and hybrid collaboration improve reach and delivery? | Candidate/client task research, withdrawn bids or applications, adjustment effectiveness and delivery rework | Standardise effective practices; protect disclosure and check the environmental effects of travel choices separately |
| Manufacturer | Can repairable, usable products and accessible return routes extend product life and serve more customers? | Repair success, material flows, customer burden, worker safety and lifecycle costs | Scale repair capability where value and environmental results are demonstrated; avoid shifting work to unsafe suppliers |
| Public service or charity | Can accessible multichannel eligibility and applications improve suitable uptake? | Unmet need, completion, comprehension, waiting time, errors, appeals and cost per successful outcome | Improve routes that reduce exclusion; preserve essential support and do not equate digital adoption with success |
| Infrastructure or energy provider | Can a transition service distribute benefits fairly and withstand disruption? | Location-specific uptake, affordability, accessible use, continuity and measured environmental results | Adjust siting, channels, finance or support; evaluate who bears the burden as well as total uptake |

Across settings, ask whether the intervention produces equivalent value, whether benefits reach people previously excluded, and whether environmental or operational gains are achieved by transferring costs to them.

## 16. Test whether the model is useful

At each strategic review, check:

- Can we trace a priority from organisational purpose through evidence to a funded decision?
- Have affected people influenced the design and assessed the results?
- Are severe impacts addressed even where the financial case is weak?
- Can we identify both environmental performance and who receives its benefits or bears its costs?
- Do we understand differences in experience that organisation-wide averages hide?
- Do our metrics include people who abandon, withdraw or cannot reach the service?
- Have we distinguished commitments, activities, capability, outcomes and evidence quality?
- Do opportunity cases explain value mechanisms and test alternative explanations?
- Are costs, capacity, cash, mission value and environmental units kept distinct?
- Are supplier and community impacts included where relevant?
- Can an owner act on each significant issue, and can people obtain remedy?
- Are external claims bounded, reviewed and traceable?

Answer with evidence and next decisions. Do not convert the checklist into an unvalidated maturity score. If the model produces extensive reporting without changing investments, design or experiences, simplify the reporting and strengthen decision ownership.
